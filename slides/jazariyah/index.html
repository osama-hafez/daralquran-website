The Author — the man الإمام ابن الجزري

Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī ibn Yūsuf al-ʿUmarī al-Dimashqī. Laqab: Shams al-Dīn. Kunyah: Abū al-Khayr.

“al-Jazarī” comes from jazīrah, a peninsula — most experts say Jazīrat Ibn ʿUmar.

Born of a duʿāʾ at Zamzam

His father, a merchant, spent forty years longing for a son and made duʿāʾ at the well of Zamzam during ḥajj. Ibn al-Jazarī was born on a Saturday night, 25 Ramaḍān 751 AH, just after the Tarāwīḥ prayer.

Ḥifẓ of the whole Qurʾān at 13. Leading the community in ṣalāh at 14.

Died 5 Rabīʿ al-Awwal 833 AH, a Friday, in Shīrāz — buried in the school he founded there.

The Author — the scholar شيخ القرّاء

Studied the seven qirāʾāt in the Levant, then the Ḥijāz, then Egypt under Ibn al-Sāʾigh, Ibn al-Baghdādī and Ibn al-Jundī. While reading to Ibn al-Jundī he reached Sūrah al-Naḥl v. 90 — and his teacher died.

Titles and posts

Imām al-Muqriʾīn · Shaykh al-Qurrāʾ. Succeeded his teacher Ibn al-Sallār at the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, and founded a Dār al-Qurʾān there.

Taught seven years in Rome under Sultan Bā Yazīd. Taymur seated him ahead of al-Sayyid al-Sharīf al-Jurjānī — “one more learned in the Qurʾān and Sunnah takes the closer seat.”

His family

All his children were ḥuffāẓ. His daughter Salmā recited the entire Qurʾān to him from memory in the ten readings in 832 AH.

Lesson 1 · verses 1–8

Before verse 1: the basmalah and the ḥamd البسملة والحمد

Two aḥādīth stand behind the poem’s opening: every good action not begun with bismillāh — and every good action not begun with the praise of Allah — is severed of blessing.

Is there a contradiction?

No. An action has an actual start and a relative start. It is like beginning a khatm: the actual start is the istiʿādhah, yet reaching al-ḥamdu lillāh is still “the beginning.”

al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm

Both come from raḥmah, and both are intensive forms. More letters means more intensity — so al-Raḥmān is the stronger. It is used of Allah alone; al-Raḥīm is also used of creation (Tawbah 128, of the Prophet ﷺ).

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ

The poem opens where

the Qurʾān opens.

Why does he name himself before the ḥamd? عِلْمٌ نَقْلِيّ

on verse 1

It looks out of order. Convention is to praise Allah first — yet the poet’s name comes in the very first word.

Knowledge is of two kinds

ʿIlm ʿaqlī — a science a person can reach by intellect alone, without a teacher.

ʿIlm naqlī — a science that cannot be reached by intellect; it exists only as a transmission handed from one person to the next.

Tajwīd is naqlī

So the author names the nāqil — the transmitter — first. The science is only as sound as the chain that carries it.

The lesson for the student is immediate: you cannot get this from a book. Find a teacher.

Lesson 2 · verses 9–19

What a makhraj is — and how to find one تعريف المخرج

Makhraj: the place from which something comes out. Technically — the place where the sound of a letter ends. Plural: makhārij.

Ḥarf itself means “end” or “point”: the letter is what you hear when the sound terminates.

The method

Make the letter sākin, or better, mushaddad. Put a hamzah with a ḥarakah before it. Then feel where the sound stops — that is its makhraj.

Two kinds

Muḥaqqaq — definite: the throat, the tongue, the lips.

Muqaddar — approximate: the jawf, the empty space of mouth and throat.

Makhraj 1 — al-Jawf الجوف

verse 10

The jawf is the empty space of the mouth and throat. From it come the three letters of madd: the alif, the wāw sākinah after a ḍammah, and the yāʾ sākinah after a kasrah.

Why the alif is the cardinal letter

Madd is always made in the alif. Its two “sisters” lose it the moment they move — and then they take a completely different makhraj.

“Ending in the air”

The sound of these letters does not in fact stop in the jawf; it carries on and stops outside the mouth. Khalīl still counts the jawf as their makhraj because their sound depends so heavily on the cavity of the mouth. They are, compared to other letters, weak.

Makhārij 10–11 — and how ل ن ر differ النون والراء

verse 15

10 · The Nūn

Found lower than the makhraj of the lām: the tip of the tongue touches the gums of the upper central incisors.

11 · The Rāʾ

Close to the nūn — but the top of the tongue is brought in as well, set further back, as it touches the same gums.

The disagreement

Al-Farrāʾ counts lām, nūn and rāʾ as one makhraj. Most scholars distinguish them, and the distinction is exact:

The lām adds the anterior sides of the tongue. The rāʾ adds the top of the tongue. The nūn has neither. Three letters, three makhārij.

Lesson 5 · verses 34–40

Tafkhīm and tarqīq التفخيم والترقيق

Tafkhīm literally means to make something fat or full — technically, the fullness that fills the mouth on certain letters. Tarqīq means to make thin — the flatness found in the mouth on the rest.

Why a whole chapter on the light ones

Reciters attend carefully to the heavy letters and neglect the light ones — which end up sounding slightly thick, because they are surrounded by heavy letters that colour them.

Five letters, but one rule

Ibn al-Jazarī illustrates with the alif, hamzah, lām, mīm and bāʾ. That does not confine the rule to them: it applies to every light letter, throughout the Qurʾān.

Three letters are set aside from “always light,” each with its own chapter: the alif, the rāʾ, and the lām of the Name.

تفخيم ↔ ترقيق

full ↔ flat

The neglected half

is the flat one.

The alif question مسألة الألف

verse 34

The alif follows the letter before it — heavy after heavy, light after light. So how can the poem say it is never read with tafkhīm?

Two weak answers

“He means the hamzah.” “He means the alif in the ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt.” Mullā ʿAlī al-Qārī labels both far-fetched.

Abū Bakr ibn al-Jazarī — restrict it

The author’s own son: read the statement as applying to the alif after a light letter, where it will indeed never be heavy.

Take it generally. The author was correcting certain non-Arabs who were exaggerating the alif after a full-mouth letter. It was also the position of his teacher Ibn al-Jundī, and of al-Jaʿbarī before him.

In al-Nashr, Ibn al-Jazarī reports the majority view and calls “the alif is never read with tafkhīm” incorrect. The Muqaddimah likely preserves his earlier, teacher’s position.

Two things students get wrong تنبيهان

verses 77–78

Ibtidāʾ is always chosen

Waqf can be forced on you. Starting never is — you always pick the place.

Which is why ibtidāʾ is graded the same four ways. A ḥasan stop can be followed by a qabīḥ start, and a qabīḥ stop by a ḥasan one.

No stop is compulsory or forbidden

Nobody sins for stopping, or for continuing, at a given place.

Except where there is a reason — someone who understands the meaning and stops deliberately, or in jest, to distort it.

So when a teacher says “stop here” or “go through,” they mean it is better in their judgement. Not that it is obligatory.

Maqṭūʿ — compounds written apart. Mawṣūl — compounds written joined.

If a compound is joined, you may only stop at the end of the whole word. If it is cut, you may stop on either part.

The wāw test

how to tell them apart

Prefix a wāw to the word.

If the hamzah disappears, it is waṣl — وَاسْتَغْفِرْ.

Lesson 17 · verses 104–105

Three ways to stop كيفيّة الوقف

verses 104–105

Iskān

الإسكان

the default

Drop the ḥarakah entirely and give the last letter a plain sukūn.

Allowed on any ending — fatḥah, ḍammah or kasrah.

نَسْتَعِينْ

Rawm

الرّوم

hinting the vowel

Pronounce about a third of the ḥarakah — the voice lowered so far that only someone close to you hears it.

Allowed on a kasrah and a ḍammah. Not on a fatḥah.

الرَّحِيمِ

Ishmām

الإشمام

a silent gesture

Round the lips toward a ḍammah immediately after the sukūn is read.

No sound is produced at all — it is seen, not heard.

Only on a ḍammah or rafʿ.

نَسْتَعِينُ

Fatḥ vs. naṣb, ḍamm vs. rafʿ: the first of each pair names a word that is mabnī (undeclinable), the second one that is muʿrab (declinable). Rawm is barred on both fatḥ and naṣb.

وَحَاذِرِ الْوَقْفَ بِكُلِّ الْحَرَكَهْ
And beware of pausing with a full ḥarakah,
إِلَّا إِذَا رُمْتَ فَبَعْضُ الْحَرَكَهْ
except when you make rawm — then part of the ḥarakah,
إِلَّا بِفَتْحٍ أَوْ بِنَصْبٍ وَأَشِمْ
except on a fatḥah or naṣb; and apply ishmām
إِشَارَةً بِالضَّمِّ فِي رَفْعٍ وَضَمّ
by pointing to a ḍammah on a word of rafʿ or ḍamm.

Try it تطبيق

verses 104–105

Take three word-endings and work through what each one permits.

A ḍammah ending

نَسْتَعِينُ — iskān, or ishmām. Rawm is also open to you here.

A kasrah ending

الرَّحِيمِ — iskān, or rawm. Ishmām is not available: the lips only round toward a ḍammah.

A fatḥah ending

الْعَالَمِينَ — iskān only. Neither rawm nor ishmām applies to a fatḥah.

The general rule underneath all three: never stop with a full ḥarakah.
